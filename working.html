I
Modelling

Roads become a graph

OpenStreetMap stores every campus road as a list of GPS points. Each pair of neighbouring points becomes an edge, and its weight is the real distance between them on the Earth's surface, worked out with the haversine formula.

II
Representation

Every vertex remembers its neighbours

A campus graph is sparse: a junction touches two or three paths, never hundreds. So instead of a V×V matrix that would be mostly empty, NAKSH keeps an adjacency list. Each vertex holds only the edges it actually has, so memory is O(V + E).

III
Data structure

The min-heap always knows what's closest

Dijkstra keeps asking one question: which unsettled place is nearest? A binary min-heap answers it in O(log n). It's a tree stored inside a flat array: the parent of index i sits at ⌊(i−1)/2⌋, and no parent is ever larger than its children.

IV
The algorithm

Dijkstra, one decision at a time

Take the nearest unsettled place from the heap and lock its distance: nothing found later can beat it, because every edge length is positive. Then try every path leading out of it. If going through it is shorter, write the new distance down and remember where you came from. Repeat until the destination is locked.

V
Analysis

Why it answers in a millisecond

With a heap, each vertex is settled once and each edge is relaxed at most twice, and every heap operation costs O(log V). Without a heap, picking the nearest vertex means scanning all of them every time.
